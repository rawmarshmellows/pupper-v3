# Subatomic Particles

> **Related:** [[quick-context/atoms-molecules-polymers-basics]] | [[quick-context/covalent-bonds]] | [[quick-context/electric-current]] | [[quick-context/doped-silicon]]

> **TL;DR:** Atoms are made of protons (positive, in nucleus), neutrons (neutral, in nucleus), and electrons (negative, orbiting)—protons and neutrons are themselves made of quarks bound by the strong force, while electrons are fundamental particles; the number of protons defines what element you have, and electron behavior explains all of chemistry.

## The Core Problem

Everything around you—your body, your computer, the air you breathe—is made of atoms. But what makes a carbon atom different from an oxygen atom? What holds an atom together? Why do some atoms bond while others don't? The answers lie in subatomic structure. If protons, neutrons, and electrons didn't exist with their specific properties, matter as we know it wouldn't exist. The positive protons and negative electrons create the electromagnetic forces that [[quick-context/covalent-bonds|hold atoms together in molecules]]. The number of protons determines which element you're looking at (change the proton count and you've transmuted one element into another). [[quick-context/electric-current|Electric current]] is literally the flow of electrons. Understanding subatomic particles is the foundation for everything from [[quick-context/doped-silicon|semiconductor electronics]] to [[quick-context/electrolysis|electrochemistry]] to nuclear power.

## 5 Essential Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Proton** | A positively charged particle in the nucleus; its count defines the element (1 proton = hydrogen, 6 = carbon, 79 = gold). Mass: ~1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. Charge: +1 elementary charge. |
| **Neutron** | A neutral particle in the nucleus; adds mass without changing element identity. Same mass as proton. Different neutron counts create isotopes (same element, different mass). |
| **Electron** | A negatively charged particle that orbits the nucleus; participates in [[quick-context/covalent-bonds|chemical bonding]] and [[quick-context/electric-current|electrical conduction]]. Mass: ~9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg (~1/1836 of a proton). Charge: -1 elementary charge. |
| **Quark** | A fundamental particle that makes up protons and neutrons. Two types matter here: up quarks (+2/3 charge) and down quarks (-1/3 charge). Proton = 2 up + 1 down; neutron = 1 up + 2 down. |
| **Strong Force** | The force that binds quarks into protons/neutrons and binds protons/neutrons into nuclei. ~100× stronger than [[learning/notes/quick-context/electromagnetism|electromagnetism]] but only works at nuclear distances (~10⁻¹⁵ m). |

## Atomic Structure: The Planetary Model (Simplified)

The atom has two regions: a dense central nucleus containing protons and neutrons, and a much larger outer region where electrons exist. The nucleus is incredibly small—if an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be a marble at the center. Yet the nucleus contains 99.97% of the atom's mass.

```
THE ATOM: A MATTER OF SCALE
================================================================================

          ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
          │                                                             │
          │                    ELECTRON CLOUD                           │
          │               (where electrons "orbit")                     │
          │                                                             │
          │                          ∙                                  │
          │               ∙                    ∙                        │
          │         ∙                                ∙                  │
          │                                                             │
          │       ∙              ┌───┐                ∙                 │
          │                      │ N │                                  │
          │      ∙           ┌───┤   ├───┐             ∙                │
          │                  │ P │ P │ N │                              │
          │       ∙          └───┤   ├───┘            ∙                 │
          │                      │ P │                                  │
          │         ∙            └───┘              ∙                   │
          │                     NUCLEUS                                 │
          │               ∙                    ∙                        │
          │                          ∙                                  │
          │                                                             │
          └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

    P = Proton (+1 charge)      Scale comparison:
    N = Neutron (0 charge)      • Atom diameter: ~10⁻¹⁰ m (100 picometers)
    ∙ = Electron (-1 charge)    • Nucleus diameter: ~10⁻¹⁵ m (1 femtometer)
                                • Ratio: 100,000:1 (stadium to marble)

    The electron cloud is NOT a fixed orbit—electrons exist as probability
    distributions (quantum mechanics). But for chemistry, the "shell" model works.


THE THREE PARTICLES COMPARED:
================================================================================

    Property        │ PROTON          │ NEUTRON         │ ELECTRON
    ════════════════╪═════════════════╪═════════════════╪═════════════════
    Location        │ Nucleus         │ Nucleus         │ Orbiting nucleus
    Charge          │ +1              │ 0               │ -1
    Mass (kg)       │ 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷   │ 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷   │ 9.109 × 10⁻³¹
    Relative mass   │ ~1              │ ~1              │ ~1/1836
    Made of         │ 2 up + 1 down   │ 1 up + 2 down   │ Fundamental
                    │ quarks          │ quarks          │ (not made of
                    │                 │                 │ smaller parts)
    ────────────────┼─────────────────┼─────────────────┼─────────────────
    Role            │ Defines element │ Adds mass,      │ Determines
                    │ identity        │ stabilizes      │ chemistry and
                    │                 │ nucleus         │ bonding
```

## What Defines an Element?

The number of protons—called the **atomic number**—is the identity card of an element. Change the proton count, and you change the element entirely:

```
PROTON COUNT DEFINES THE ELEMENT
================================================================================

    Protons │ Element  │ Symbol │ What it is
    ════════╪══════════╪════════╪════════════════════════════════════════════
        1   │ Hydrogen │   H    │ Simplest atom. One proton, one electron.
        2   │ Helium   │   He   │ Noble gas. Won't bond with anything.
        6   │ Carbon   │   C    │ Basis of organic chemistry, life, polymers.
        7   │ Nitrogen │   N    │ 78% of air. Essential for proteins.
        8   │ Oxygen   │   O    │ 21% of air. Essential for respiration.
       14   │ Silicon  │   Si   │ Basis of semiconductors and electronics.
       26   │ Iron     │   Fe   │ Core of Earth. Hemoglobin. Steel.
       29   │ Copper   │   Cu   │ Excellent conductor. Wiring.
       79   │ Gold     │   Au   │ Noble metal. Corrosion-resistant.
       92   │ Uranium  │   U    │ Heaviest natural element. Nuclear fuel.

    Add or remove a proton → DIFFERENT ELEMENT
    Add or remove a neutron → Same element, different ISOTOPE
    Add or remove an electron → Same element, different ION


NEUTRONS CREATE ISOTOPES (same element, different mass):
================================================================================

    Carbon-12 (stable)          Carbon-14 (radioactive)
    ──────────────────          ─────────────────────────
    6 protons (defines C)       6 protons (still carbon!)
    6 neutrons                  8 neutrons
    Mass number = 12            Mass number = 14

    Both are carbon. Both have 6 protons. Both have same chemistry.
    But C-14 is unstable—it decays over time (used for carbon dating).


ELECTRONS CREATE IONS (same element, different charge):
================================================================================

    Neutral sodium (Na)         Sodium ion (Na⁺)
    ───────────────────         ─────────────────
    11 protons                  11 protons (still sodium!)
    11 electrons                10 electrons (lost one)
    Net charge: 0               Net charge: +1

    Still sodium. Still 11 protons. But now it's an ion—
    it will behave differently in solution and bonding.
```

## Quarks: Going Deeper

Protons and neutrons aren't fundamental—they're made of smaller particles called **quarks**, held together by the **strong force** (mediated by particles called gluons). This is the realm of particle physics.

```
INSIDE PROTONS AND NEUTRONS: QUARKS
================================================================================

    There are 6 types ("flavors") of quarks, but only 2 matter for normal matter:

    • UP QUARK (u):   charge = +2/3
    • DOWN QUARK (d): charge = -1/3


    PROTON = 2 up + 1 down
    ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════

             ┌───────────────────────┐
             │                       │
             │    u         u        │     Charge calculation:
             │   +2/3     +2/3       │     (+2/3) + (+2/3) + (-1/3)
             │         d             │     = +4/3 - 1/3
             │       -1/3            │     = +3/3
             │                       │     = +1 ✓
             └───────────────────────┘


    NEUTRON = 1 up + 2 down
    ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════

             ┌───────────────────────┐
             │                       │
             │    d         d        │     Charge calculation:
             │   -1/3     -1/3       │     (+2/3) + (-1/3) + (-1/3)
             │         u             │     = +2/3 - 2/3
             │       +2/3            │     = 0 ✓
             │                       │
             └───────────────────────┘


    WHY CAN'T WE SEE INDIVIDUAL QUARKS?
    ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    The strong force has a bizarre property: it gets STRONGER as quarks
    separate. Try to pull a quark out of a proton, and the energy you
    add creates NEW quark-antiquark pairs that immediately bind.

    You can never have a free quark. This is called "confinement."

    The strong force is carried by "gluons"—like photons carry
    electromagnetic force, but gluons themselves have "color charge"
    and interact with each other, making the physics extremely complex.
```

## Why the Nucleus Doesn't Fly Apart

Here's a puzzle: protons are all positively charged. Positive charges repel each other (electromagnetism). So why doesn't the nucleus explode?

```
THE NUCLEAR STABILITY PUZZLE
================================================================================

    ELECTROMAGNETIC FORCE (repulsion):
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

         ⊕ ←────────────────────────────────────────────→ ⊕
         P                  REPULSION                      P

    Protons repel each other. This force is significant and has
    infinite range (weakening as 1/r²).


    STRONG FORCE (attraction):
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

         ⊕ ←══════════════════════════════════════════════→ ⊕
         P               STRONG ATTRACTION                   P

    The strong force pulls nucleons (protons AND neutrons) together.
    It's ~100× stronger than electromagnetism, BUT:
    • Only works at very short range (~10⁻¹⁵ m)
    • Beyond this distance, it drops to essentially zero


    HOW NEUTRONS HELP:
    ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    Small nucleus (e.g., Helium-4):      Large nucleus (e.g., Lead-208):

         ┌─────────┐                          ┌─────────────────┐
         │  P   N  │                          │ P N P N P N P N │
         │  N   P  │                          │ N P N P N P N P │
         └─────────┘                          │ P N P N P N P N │
                                              │ N P N P N P N P │
    2 protons, 2 neutrons                     │ ...many more... │
    Ratio: 1:1                                └─────────────────┘

                                              82 protons, 126 neutrons
                                              Ratio: 1:1.54

    • Neutrons add strong-force attraction without adding charge repulsion
    • Small nuclei: ~1:1 ratio works
    • Large nuclei: need MORE neutrons to overcome increasing proton repulsion
    • Above ~83 protons: no stable configuration exists → all heavy elements
      are radioactive
```

## How This Connects to Electronics and Chemistry

The electrons are where the action is for chemistry and electronics:

```
ELECTRONS: THE CHEMISTRY AND ELECTRONICS PARTICLE
================================================================================

    CHEMISTRY (covalent bonding):
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    Atoms "want" full outer electron shells (usually 8 electrons).
    They achieve this by SHARING electrons with neighbors.

         H atom             H atom                H₂ molecule
         (1 electron)       (1 electron)          (shared pair)

            ∙                  ∙                    ∙──∙
            H                  H                    H   H

    This is [[quick-context/covalent-bonds|covalent bonding]]—the basis of
    molecules, polymers, and all of organic chemistry.


    ELECTRICAL CONDUCTION:
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    In metals, outer electrons are loosely held—they can move freely.
    Apply voltage → electrons flow → [[quick-context/electric-current|electric current]].

    ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │  Metal conductor (e.g., copper wire)                            │
    │                                                                 │
    │   ⊕    ⊕    ⊕    ⊕    ⊕    ⊕    ⊕    ⊕    ⊕    Copper nuclei │
    │     e⁻ → e⁻ → e⁻ → e⁻ → e⁻ → e⁻ → e⁻ → e⁻ →    (fixed in     │
    │   ⊕    ⊕    ⊕    ⊕    ⊕    ⊕    ⊕    ⊕    ⊕     lattice)      │
    │                                                                 │
    │   Free electrons flow when voltage applied                      │
    └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘


    SEMICONDUCTORS ([[quick-context/doped-silicon|doped silicon]]):
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    Silicon holds its electrons more tightly than metals.
    Add impurities (doping) to create:
    • N-type: extra electrons (negative charge carriers)
    • P-type: missing electrons ("holes" = positive charge carriers)

    The junction between N and P creates a one-way valve for current—
    the basis of diodes and transistors.


    ELECTROLYSIS AND BATTERIES:
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    In [[quick-context/electrolysis|electrolysis]], electrons are forced to move
    where they don't naturally want to go, driving chemical reactions.

    In [[quick-context/galvanic-cells-batteries|batteries]], electrons naturally
    flow from one material to another, and we capture that flow as current.

    All of this is electrons moving between atoms.
```

## Classical vs. Quantum: Two Ways to Think About Atoms

**Classical (Bohr model):** Electrons orbit the nucleus like planets around the sun, in fixed circular paths at specific energy levels. Simple, intuitive, and useful for basic chemistry—but fundamentally wrong.

**Quantum (modern):** Electrons don't have definite positions; they exist as probability clouds (orbitals) where they're likely to be found. The electron is "everywhere at once" until measured. This is mathematically correct but deeply counterintuitive.

```
THE TWO MODELS OF ATOMIC STRUCTURE
================================================================================

    BOHR MODEL (1913)                    QUANTUM MODEL (1926+)
    ─────────────────                    ─────────────────────

         Shell 3 ○                           Probability cloud
               ↘                             (electron orbital)
         Shell 2  ○
                ↘                              ░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░
         Shell 1   ○                          ░░░░▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓░░░░░
                  ↘                           ░░▓▓▓▓████▓▓▓▓░░░
              ┌────⊕────┐                     ░▓▓▓██████▓▓▓░░
              │ NUCLEUS │                     ░▓▓██●NUCLEUS██▓▓░
              └─────────┘                     ░▓▓▓██████▓▓▓░░
                                              ░░▓▓▓▓████▓▓▓▓░░░
    Electrons in fixed orbits               ░░░░▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓░░░░░
    at specific radii                         ░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░

    GOOD FOR:                              GOOD FOR:
    • Basic chemistry intuition            • Accurate physics
    • Explaining electron shells           • Molecular orbitals
    • Understanding bonding                • Spectroscopy
    • First approximation                  • Magnetic properties

    WRONG ABOUT:                           COUNTERINTUITIVE:
    • Electrons having fixed paths         • "Probability" is fundamental
    • Electrons as point particles         • Electron is "everywhere"
    • Details of orbital shapes            • Wave-particle duality


    FOR MOST PRACTICAL PURPOSES (chemistry, electronics):
    ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    The shell model (simplified Bohr) works fine:

    • Electrons fill shells in order
    • First shell holds 2, second holds 8, third holds 18...
    • Outer shell electrons determine chemical behavior
    • Full outer shell = stable (noble gases)
    • Partially full outer shell = reactive (will bond)
```

| Situation | Use This Model | Why |
|-----------|---------------|-----|
| Understanding electron shells | Bohr (classical) | Simple, explains periodic table |
| Predicting chemical bonding | Shell model | Outer electrons determine reactivity |
| Understanding [[quick-context/doped-silicon\|semiconductors]] | Band theory (quantum) | Energy bands, not orbits |
| Atomic spectroscopy | Quantum model | Energy levels aren't simple circles |
| Molecular orbitals | Quantum model | Orbitals overlap and hybridize |

## Building Atoms: From Hydrogen to Carbon

Let's build up some common atoms particle by particle:

```
BUILDING ATOMS — PARTICLE BY PARTICLE
================================================================================

HYDROGEN (H) — The Simplest Atom
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    Atomic number: 1 (1 proton)

    Nucleus:    ⊕         Just one proton
                ↑
              1 proton
              (1 up, 1 up, 1 down quarks inside)

    Electron:    ∙         One electron orbiting
                 │
                 └── balances the proton's +1 charge

    Result: Electrically neutral atom

    This is the most abundant element in the universe.
    Most of the Sun is hydrogen.


HELIUM (He) — First Noble Gas
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    Atomic number: 2 (2 protons)

    Nucleus:   ⊕ ⊖        2 protons + 2 neutrons
               ⊖ ⊕        (⊖ = neutron)

    Electrons:   ∙ ∙      2 electrons (fills first shell!)
                 └─┘
              Full shell = STABLE = won't bond with anything

    This is why helium is used in balloons—it's completely inert.


CARBON (C) — The Element of Life
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    Atomic number: 6 (6 protons)

                                  Second shell
                               (holds up to 8, has 4)
                                    ∙   ∙
                                   ╱     ╲
    Nucleus:   ⊕⊖⊕              ∙         ∙    First shell (full: 2)
               ⊖⊖⊖             │ ⊕⊖⊕       │
               ⊕⊖⊕              ∙ ⊖⊖⊖ ∙
                                   ╲ ⊕⊖⊕╱
    6 protons                       ∙   ∙
    6 neutrons

    4 electrons in outer shell → can form 4 bonds!

    This 4-bond capability makes carbon uniquely suited for:
    • Long chains (polymers, plastics)
    • Complex molecules (DNA, proteins)
    • All organic chemistry


SILICON (Si) — The Element of Electronics
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    Atomic number: 14 (14 protons)

    Shell 1: 2 electrons (full)
    Shell 2: 8 electrons (full)
    Shell 3: 4 electrons (same as carbon!)

    Silicon is directly below carbon in the periodic table.
    Same outer structure (4 electrons) → similar bonding behavior.

    But silicon's electrons are held less tightly than carbon's.
    This makes silicon a SEMICONDUCTOR:
    • Not as conductive as metal
    • Not as insulating as glass
    • Conductivity controllable by [[quick-context/doped-silicon|doping]]

    This is why silicon is the basis of all computer chips.


COPPER (Cu) — The Conductor
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    Atomic number: 29 (29 protons)

    Most shells: completely full
    Outer shell: 1 electron (loosely held)

    That single outer electron is almost free—it barely
    feels the pull of the nucleus.

    Apply voltage → electrons flow easily → excellent conductor

    Copper has the second-best electrical conductivity
    (after silver, which is too expensive for most uses).
```

## Example: Why Sodium Explodes in Water

Understanding atomic structure explains violent chemical reactions:

```
SODIUM IN WATER — A Subatomic Explanation
================================================================================

    SODIUM (Na) — Atomic number: 11
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    Shell 1: 2 electrons ✓ (full)
    Shell 2: 8 electrons ✓ (full)
    Shell 3: 1 electron   ← This one wants to LEAVE

    Sodium "wants" to lose that outer electron to have a full outer shell.
    It takes very little energy to remove it.


    WATER (H₂O)
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    The O-H bonds are polar: oxygen pulls electrons toward itself.
    Water molecules can accept electrons.


    THE REACTION:
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    1. Sodium hits water

    2. Sodium's outer electron jumps to water molecules:
       Na → Na⁺ + e⁻

    3. Water splits, using that electron:
       2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻

    4. The hydrogen gas is released so fast, and the reaction
       is so exothermic, that the hydrogen IGNITES.

    BOOM! 💥

    All because sodium has ONE loosely-held electron that
    desperately wants to escape.


    CONTRAST WITH COPPER:
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    Copper also has 1 outer electron, but:
    • It's held more tightly (higher nuclear charge, d-orbital effects)
    • Copper is "noble enough" to not spontaneously react with water
    • You can have copper pipes carrying water indefinitely

    The energy required to remove that electron matters enormously.
```

**The one thing most outsiders get wrong about this is...** thinking electrons orbit the nucleus like planets orbit the sun. They don't. Electrons exist as probability clouds—the electron is statistically more likely to be found in certain regions, but it doesn't trace a definite path. This matters because it explains why atoms can bond (electron clouds overlap), why quantum tunneling works (electrons can "teleport" through barriers), and why semiconductor physics requires quantum mechanics. For basic chemistry, the planetary model is a useful lie. For understanding modern electronics at a fundamental level, you need the quantum picture.

<summary><strong>Peripheral Knowledge</strong></summary>

- **[[quick-context/atoms-molecules-polymers-basics]]** — Builds on atomic structure to explain how atoms combine into molecules and polymers. The electron behavior described here is what enables covalent bonding.

- **[[quick-context/covalent-bonds]]** — The sharing of electrons between atoms to form molecules. Understanding electron shells explains why carbon forms 4 bonds, oxygen forms 2, hydrogen forms 1.

- **[[quick-context/doped-silicon]]** — Manipulating electron counts at the atomic level (adding dopants) to create controllable semiconductors. The free electrons and holes are literally extra or missing electrons relative to pure silicon.

- **[[quick-context/electric-current]]** — Current is flowing electrons. Understanding that electrons are tiny, negatively charged particles explains why conductors work (loose outer electrons) and insulators don't (tightly held electrons).

- **[[quick-context/electrolysis]]** — Using electricity to force electron transfer that wouldn't happen spontaneously. Electrons move from [[learning/notes/micro-context/cathode|cathode]] to ions in solution, enabling chemical transformations.

- **[[quick-context/galvanic-cells-batteries]]** — Batteries harvest electron flow from spontaneous chemical reactions. The electrode potential differences come from how strongly different atoms hold their electrons.

- **Radioactivity and Nuclear Physics** — When the nucleus is unstable (too many or too few neutrons), it decays by emitting particles. Alpha decay, beta decay, and gamma radiation all involve subatomic particle transformations.

- **The Standard Model** — The complete theory of fundamental particles: 6 quarks, 6 leptons (including the electron), and force-carrying bosons (photons, gluons, W/Z bosons, Higgs). Everything we've discussed is the "low-energy" corner of this theory.

**Q1:** Carbon has 6 protons. If you could somehow add one more proton to a carbon nucleus, what would you have?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
**Nitrogen.** The number of protons defines the element. Carbon (6 protons) → Nitrogen (7 protons). This is nuclear transmutation—changing one element into another. It doesn't happen in chemical reactions (which only involve electrons); it requires nuclear reactions. See: "What Defines an Element?" in How It Works.
</details>

**Q2:** A proton has charge +1. It's made of 2 up quarks and 1 down quark. Show that the quark charges add up correctly.
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
**Up quark: +2/3. Down quark: -1/3.** For a proton (uud): (+2/3) + (+2/3) + (-1/3) = +4/3 - 1/3 = +3/3 = **+1**. The fractional charges of quarks always combine to give integer charges for composite particles. Neutrons (udd): (+2/3) + (-1/3) + (-1/3) = +2/3 - 2/3 = **0**. See: "Quarks: Going Deeper" diagram.
</details>

**Q3:** Why do heavier elements need proportionally more neutrons than lighter elements to remain stable?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
**Electromagnetic repulsion increases faster than strong force attraction.** As more protons pack into the nucleus, each proton repels every other proton (electromagnetic force has infinite range). But the strong force only attracts immediate neighbors (very short range). With more protons, you need more neutrons to add strong-force "glue" without adding more repulsion. Above ~83 protons, no configuration is stable enough—all very heavy elements are radioactive. See: "Why the Nucleus Doesn't Fly Apart."
</details>

**Q4:** Copper and sodium both have 1 electron in their outer shell. Why does sodium explode in water while copper is safe for water pipes?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
**Sodium's outer electron is much easier to remove.** While both have 1 outer electron, sodium's electron is in the 3rd shell far from a nucleus with only +11 charge. Copper's electron is effectively in a higher shell but with +29 nuclear charge (and d-orbital effects make it even harder to remove). The ionization energy (energy to remove that electron) is much lower for sodium, making it violently reactive. See: "Why Sodium Explodes in Water" example.
</details>

**Q5:** How does understanding subatomic particles help explain why [[quick-context/doped-silicon|doping silicon]] creates n-type and p-type semiconductors?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
**Doping adds or removes electrons at the atomic level.** Pure silicon has 4 outer electrons, all used for bonding with neighbors. Adding phosphorus (5 outer electrons) provides one extra electron that's free to conduct—this is n-type (negative charge carriers). Adding boron (3 outer electrons) creates a "hole" (missing electron) that acts like a positive charge carrier—this is p-type. Understanding that electrical properties come from electron counts, and that we can surgically modify those counts by adding specific atoms, explains how semiconductor devices work.
</details>
