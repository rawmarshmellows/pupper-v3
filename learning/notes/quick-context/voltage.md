# Voltage

> **Related:** [[micro-context/capacitive-voltage-sensing]] | [[micro-context/input-offset-voltage]] | [[micro-context/offset-voltage-drift]] | [[micro-context/open-loop-voltage-gain]] | [[micro-context/output-voltage-swing]]

> **TL;DR:** Voltage is the electric potential difference between two points—defined by the electric field between them. The field exerts force on charges, and in a conductive material, a stronger field drives more current (with the exact relationship depending on the material's resistance).

## The Core Problem

You have a battery, and you want current to flow through a wire to do useful work. But what actually *causes* electrons to move? The answer is the **electric field**. When you connect a battery, it establishes an electric field inside the conductor. This field exerts force on the free electrons, pushing them in one direction. The voltage between two points is a measure of how much work the field does on a charge moving between those points—it's the *integral* of the electric field along the path. Higher voltage means a stronger field (over the same distance), which pushes harder on electrons, which—depending on the material's resistance—results in more [[quick-context/electric-current|current]].

## 5 Essential Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Volt (V)** | The unit of voltage. 1 volt = 1 joule of energy per [[micro-context/coulomb-history|coulomb]] of charge. A AA battery: 1.5V. USB: 5V. Wall outlet (US): 120V. Car battery: 12V. |
| **Electric Field (E)** | A vector field that exerts force on electric charges. Measured in volts per meter (V/m). The field is what actually pushes electrons. |
| **Potential Difference** | Another name for voltage—the difference in electric potential energy per unit charge between two points. |
| **Ground/Reference** | Voltage is always relative. "Ground" is the arbitrary 0V reference point. A "9V" battery means 9V *higher than* the negative terminal. |
| **EMF (Electromotive Force)** | The voltage generated by a source (battery, generator) that creates the electric field. Technically measured in volts, not newtons. |

<details>
<summary><strong>How It Works</strong> — Electric field creates voltage, voltage drives current</summary>

The key insight: **voltage doesn't cause the electric field—the electric field defines the voltage.**

When you connect a battery, chemical reactions inside create a charge imbalance. Positive charges accumulate at one terminal, negative at the other. This charge separation creates an electric field that extends through the circuit. The voltage between any two points equals the work done by this field on a unit charge moving between those points.

```
THE ELECTRIC FIELD IS THE FUNDAMENTAL DRIVER
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

INSIDE A BATTERY (the source):
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Chemical reactions do work to separate charges:

    ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │  BATTERY                                                     │
    │                                                              │
    │   ⊕ ⊕ ⊕ ⊕ ⊕        Chemical         ⊖ ⊖ ⊖ ⊖ ⊖              │
    │   ⊕ ⊕ ⊕ ⊕ ⊕  ←──  reactions  ──→    ⊖ ⊖ ⊖ ⊖ ⊖              │
    │   ⊕ ⊕ ⊕ ⊕ ⊕        push charges     ⊖ ⊖ ⊖ ⊖ ⊖              │
    │   (+) terminal                      (-) terminal            │
    └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

    This charge separation creates an ELECTRIC FIELD between terminals.


THE FIELD IN THE WIRE:
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    (+) ────────────────── WIRE ────────────────────── (-)

         ───────────────→ Electric Field E ───────────────→

         The field points from (+) to (-).
         Field strength E = Voltage / Distance (simplified for uniform field)

    ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │  Inside the wire, the field exerts force on electrons:       │
    │                                                               │
    │      F = qE  (force = charge × field strength)               │
    │                                                               │
    │  Electrons (negative charge) feel force OPPOSITE to field:   │
    │                                                               │
    │      ←e⁻  ←e⁻  ←e⁻  ←e⁻  ←e⁻  ←e⁻  (electrons drift to +)   │
    │                                                               │
    │  This drift IS the current.                                  │
    └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘


VOLTAGE AS THE INTEGRAL OF THE FIELD:
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    Voltage between A and B = ∫(A to B) E · dl

    In plain terms: add up the field strength along the path.

    EXAMPLE: Uniform field of 10 V/m over 1 meter:

        Point A ─────────────────────────────── Point B
                  E = 10 V/m, distance = 1m

        Voltage = 10 V/m × 1 m = 10 V

    EXAMPLE: Same total voltage, shorter distance:

        Point A ──────────── Point B
                E = 100 V/m, distance = 0.1m

        Voltage = 100 V/m × 0.1 m = 10 V (same!)

    Same voltage, but STRONGER field = more force per electron.
```

```
HOW FIELD STRENGTH RELATES TO CURRENT
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The electric field pushes electrons. How much current flows depends on:
1. Field strength (more push = more drift)
2. Material properties (how easily electrons move)

INSIDE A CONDUCTOR:
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │  Copper wire: lots of free electrons, low resistance          │
    │                                                                │
    │  Field E →  →e⁻ →e⁻ →e⁻ →e⁻ →e⁻ →e⁻ →e⁻  (high current)     │
    │             electrons accelerate, collide, drift              │
    └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

    ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │  Resistive material: fewer free electrons, more collisions    │
    │                                                                │
    │  Field E →    →e⁻   →e⁻   →e⁻   →e⁻    (lower current)       │
    │              electrons impeded by structure                   │
    └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

THE RELATIONSHIP (Ohm's Law from field perspective):
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    At microscopic level:  J = σE
    (current density = conductivity × field strength)

    At circuit level:  I = V/R
    (current = voltage / resistance)

    SAME FIELD, DIFFERENT MATERIALS:

    Material      │ Conductivity │ Current for same field
    ══════════════╪══════════════╪══════════════════════════
    Copper        │ Very high    │ High current
    Iron          │ Moderate     │ Moderate current
    Silicon       │ Low (pure)   │ Low current
    Rubber        │ ~Zero        │ Essentially zero current

    Field provides the push. Material determines the response.
```

```
WHY THIS MATTERS: CORRECTING COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

WRONG: "Voltage pushes current through the wire"
RIGHT: "The electric field (defined by voltage) pushes electrons"

WRONG: "Voltage gets used up as current flows"
RIGHT: "Voltage is a difference between two points—it doesn't flow or deplete"

WRONG: "Higher voltage always means more current"
RIGHT: "Higher voltage means stronger field, which CAN drive more current
        if the material allows it (depends on resistance)"

ANALOGY REFINEMENT:
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    Common "water pressure" analogy:
    • Voltage = pressure  ✓ (sort of)
    • Current = flow rate  ✓

    But more accurate:
    • Electric FIELD = the actual pressure/force on each electron
    • Voltage = integral of that field over distance

    Like gravity: the gravitational FIELD exerts force on water.
    The "height difference" (potential) is the integral of that field.
    Height doesn't push water—gravity does. Height quantifies how much
    work gravity will do over that distance.

    Same with electricity: the electric FIELD pushes electrons.
    Voltage quantifies the work done between two points.
```

<summary><strong>The Key Tension</strong> — Voltage vs. field strength</summary>

A subtle but important distinction: voltage tells you the *total* potential difference, but field *strength* tells you the force per unit distance.

| Scenario | Voltage | Field Strength | Effect |
|----------|---------|----------------|--------|
| 1V across 1mm gap | 1V | 1000 V/m | Strong field, can cause sparks |
| 1V across 1m wire | 1V | 1 V/m | Weak field, gentle current flow |
| 10000V across 10km power line | 10kV | 1 V/m | Same weak field as above |

This is why:
- **Spark plugs** work: modest voltage (20-40kV) across tiny gap = extreme field = ionizes air
- **Power transmission** uses high voltage: same power with less current (P = IV), reducing I²R losses
- **Capacitors** can fail: thin dielectric + high voltage = field exceeds breakdown strength

The practitioner tension: high voltage is efficient for power transmission but dangerous for safety. Field strength (V/m) determines breakdown and safety, while voltage (V) determines energy delivery.

</details>

<summary><strong>Concrete Example</strong> — Field and current in a simple circuit</summary>

```
EXAMPLE: 9V BATTERY, 100Ω RESISTOR, 10CM WIRE
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │                        9V Battery                         │
    │                      (+)     (-)                          │
    └─────────────────────┬─────────┬──────────────────────────┘
                          │         │
                     10cm wire  10cm wire
                     (copper)   (copper)
                          │         │
                          └────┬────┘
                               │
                         ┌─────┴─────┐
                         │  100Ω     │
                         │ Resistor  │
                         └───────────┘


WHERE DOES THE FIELD EXIST?
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

In copper wire: Very LOW field (copper has high conductivity)
    - Wire resistance ≈ 0.001Ω for 10cm of typical wire
    - Voltage drop across wire ≈ 0.00009V (negligible)
    - Field in wire ≈ 0.0009 V/m (barely any)

In resistor: HIGH field (resistor limits current)
    - Resistor = 100Ω (almost all the resistance)
    - Voltage drop across resistor ≈ 9V (almost all of it)
    - If resistor is 1cm long: Field ≈ 900 V/m

    ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │  The electric field is NOT uniform around the circuit!       │
    │                                                              │
    │  • Wire: tiny field, electrons drift easily                  │
    │  • Resistor: strong field, electrons fight through           │
    │                                                              │
    │  Field concentrates where resistance is highest.             │
    └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘


CURRENT CALCULATION:
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    Total voltage: 9V
    Total resistance: 100Ω (wire resistance negligible)

    I = V/R = 9V / 100Ω = 0.09 A = 90 mA

    This 90mA flows through EVERY part of the circuit
    (current is conserved—same electrons, same flow rate everywhere)


THE FIELD-CURRENT RELATIONSHIP:
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    In the resistor: J = σE  →  Current density = conductivity × field

    The resistor material has low conductivity (that's what makes it
    a resistor). To maintain 90mA through it, you need a strong field.

    In the wire: same current, but copper has HIGH conductivity.
    So you need only a tiny field to push the same current through.

    ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │  Same current, different fields:                                │
    │                                                                 │
    │  Component  │ Conductivity │ Field needed │ Voltage drop       │
    │  ═══════════╪══════════════╪══════════════╪════════════════    │
    │  Copper wire│ 5.8×10⁷ S/m │ ~0.001 V/m   │ ~0.0001V           │
    │  Resistor   │ ~10⁴ S/m    │ ~900 V/m     │ ~9V                │
    │                                                                 │
    │  The field "piles up" where resistance is high.                │
    └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**The one thing most outsiders get wrong about this is...** thinking voltage is an independent "force" that exists on its own. Voltage is a *consequence* of the electric field configuration, not the cause. The battery's chemical reactions create charge separation, which creates the electric field, which is what actually pushes electrons. Voltage is our way of quantifying the total work that field does between two points. This matters because it explains why the same voltage can have very different effects depending on distance (and thus field strength): 1000V across a 1km power line is safe to work near, but 1000V across a 1mm air gap will create an arc.

<summary><strong>Test Your Understanding</strong></summary>

**Q1:** A 12V battery is connected across a 1m wire. What's the approximate electric field strength in the wire?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
**12 V/m** (assuming uniform field and ignoring resistance variations). E = V/d = 12V / 1m. In reality, most of the field would concentrate in any resistive components, with very little in the low-resistance wire itself.
</details>

**Q2:** If you double the voltage across a [[quick-context/resistor|resistor]], what happens to the electric field inside it?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
**The field doubles.** Voltage is the integral of the field over distance. If distance stays the same and voltage doubles, field must double. This doubled field exerts twice the force on electrons, which (in an ohmic material) doubles the current—consistent with I = V/R.
</details>

**Q3:** Two wires carry the same current: one copper, one nichrome (resistive). Which has the stronger electric field inside?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
**Nichrome has the stronger field.** From J = σE, current density J is the same (same current, assume same wire thickness). Nichrome has lower conductivity σ, so to maintain the same J, you need a higher E. This higher field corresponds to a higher voltage drop across the nichrome wire—which is exactly how resistors work.
</details>

**Q4:** A spark plug has a 0.5mm gap and requires 20,000V to fire. What's the field strength?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
**40 million V/m (40 MV/m).** E = V/d = 20,000V / 0.0005m = 40,000,000 V/m. This extreme field strength ionizes the air, creating a conductive plasma path (the spark). Air's breakdown strength is about 3 MV/m, so 40 MV/m easily exceeds it.
</details>

**Q5:** Why do power transmission lines use hundreds of thousands of volts but the current is relatively small?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
**To reduce I²R losses.** Power transmitted = V × I. For fixed power, you can use high V with low I, or low V with high I. The wires have resistance, causing power loss = I²R. By using high voltage (strong field pushes electrons efficiently) and low current, losses are minimized. The field strength in the wires is still low (high voltage spread over long distance), so no breakdown occurs.
</details>
